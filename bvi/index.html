Step 0

Before you fly

The BVI is a separate country (a British Overseas Territory). St. Thomas is US soil, so you cross a border on the ferry.

  1. Passports for everyone, kids included. Check they're valid well past your return date.
  2. BVI entry card (ED card) online. Fill it in at bviedcard.gov.vg within 72 hours before you arrive (The Moorings suggests 36–72 hours). You get an email with a QR code. Screenshot it for each traveler.
  3. Book the ferry. Buy round-trip tickets from Charlotte Amalie to Road Town in advance (it's cheaper and guarantees seats in holiday weeks).
  4. Charter paperwork. Moorings confirmation, approved sailing résumé, crew list, and the credit card used for the security deposit.
  5. Provisioning order (if using Moorings provisioning) is due 5 days before your charter starts.
  6. Request kid-sized life jackets when you confirm the boat, or bring your own US Coast Guard–approved ones.
  7. Download for offline: this app, a chart app with the BVI saved offline (Navionics or similar), offline Google Maps of St. Thomas and Tortola, and your entertainment.

Leg 1

St. Thomas airport → ferry

Cyril E. King Airport (STT) to the Charlotte Amalie ferry terminal is a 10–15 minute taxi ride.

  1. Collect bags and head outside to the taxi stand. Arriving from the mainland US there's no customs.
  2. Ask for "the Charlotte Amalie ferry terminal, BVI ferry to Road Town." It's the Edward Wilmoth Blyden Marine Terminal on the downtown waterfront.
  3. Agree the fare before loading. Taxis are shared vans or open-air "safaris" with set per-person rates: about $12–15 per person plus a few dollars per bag. Cash is easiest; tip 10–15%.
  4. Check in at your ferry company's counter at least 60 minutes before departure. Passports are checked; port fees and luggage charges may be collected here.
  5. Ride time is about 45–60 minutes to Road Town. December trade winds can make it bumpy, so take seasickness medicine an hour before and sit outside at the back.

Leg 2

Road Town ferry → Moorings base

The Moorings is at Wickhams Cay II, at the head of Road Harbour. It's about 1.5 miles and 5–10 minutes by taxi from the ferry dock.

  1. Immigration first. Have passports and each person's ED-card QR code ready. Pay the $10 per person levy if asked.
  2. Customs next. Bags may be opened. Declare anything you're unsure about.
  3. Taxi to "The Moorings, Wickhams Cay II." The Moorings' transfer partner Wheatley's can be pre-booked through The Moorings, and a van with a Moorings sign will be waiting.
  4. Check in at Moorings reception. Base hours are 7 AM–11 PM.

The way back

Going home

Leave a buffer of 4–5 hours between the ferry and your flight. Holiday Saturdays are the busiest.

  1. Return the boat. Charter ends at 11:00 AM. Many crews come back the afternoon before to fuel, pack, and shower.
  2. Call The Moorings on VHF 12 about 30 minutes out for docking instructions. Fenders and lines ready. They often send a dock hand.
  3. Pump out the holding tank offshore (or as instructed) before entering Road Harbour.
  4. Check out. Report any damage or breakage, hand back keys and the outboard kill cord.
  5. Ferry back to Charlotte Amalie. Typical departures from Road Town ≈ 7:30 AM, 10:00 AM, 11:30 AM and 3:30 PM (confirm). Arrive 60–90 minutes early and pay the $20 per person departure tax.
  6. US Customs (CBP) at the Charlotte Amalie terminal. Passports out.
  7. Taxi to STT (≈ $12–15 per person). There is an extra US Customs check at STT before airport security for flights to the mainland. Allow 2–3 hours at the airport.

Every morning

Engines

Daily check (each engine, before first start)

  1. Oil: dipstick between the marks.
  2. Coolant: level visible in the overflow/expansion tank.
  3. Raw-water strainer: clear of weed or plastic, seacock open.
  4. Belt: tight, not frayed or shedding black dust.
  5. Bilge and engine bay: dry, no fuel or oil smell, no new drips.
  6. After starting: water spitting from the exhaust within 30 seconds. If not, shut down and check the seacock, strainer and impeller.

Use the tick-box version →

Start and stop (typical Yanmar panel)

  • Start: throttle in neutral, key on (alarm beeps), preheat ~10 s if your panel has it, press start until it catches (no more than 10 s at a time).
  • Stop: press the stop button until the engine stops, then turn the key off. Turning the key off with the engine running can damage the alternator.
  • Before shifting into gear: look behind for lines in the water (dinghy painter, mooring lines, swim-ladder ropes). A line around the prop is the most common charter breakdown.

Fresh water & toilets

Water & heads

Fresh water

  • Tanks hold about 206 gal. A careful family uses 20–35 gal a day. Showers are the big user.
  • Navy showers: wet down, water off, soap up, quick rinse. Rinse snorkel gear in a bucket of sea water first, then a splash of fresh.
  • Turn the water-pump breaker off at night and when you leave the boat. If the pump runs with no tap open, a tank is empty or something is leaking.
  • Refill at marinas for roughly 15–25¢ a gallon. See where to fill up.
  • Watermaker (if fitted): run only in clean, clear water away from marinas and silty bays.

Marine toilets ("heads")

Nothing goes in the head unless you ate it first, plus a little toilet paper (ask whether your boat allows paper at all). No wipes, tampons, paper towels or hair.

  • Manual heads: pump 10–15 extra strokes after the bowl is clear to flush the hose. Electric: hold until clear.
  • In anchorages, marinas and near reefs, use the holding tank. Empty it only in open water well offshore, or at a pump-out, following your briefing.
  • Learn your tank's size and gauge. If a head jams, stop pumping and call the base. Forcing it makes it worse.
Kitchen

Galley & propane

  1. Gas on: switch on the propane solenoid at the panel. The tank valve in the gas locker is usually left open.
  2. Light: push and turn the knob, light it, and keep the knob pressed 5–10 seconds so the flame-safety holds.
  3. Gas off: turn the solenoid off first and let the flame burn out the line, then turn the knobs off.
Smell gas?Solenoid off. No flames, no switches. Open hatches and air out the boat. Propane is heavier than air and pools in the bilge. Call the base.
  • Know where the spare propane tank is and how to swap it. A tank usually lasts a week of normal cooking.
  • Fridge: open it briefly, pre-chill food, and keep drinks in a cooler with ice so the fridge stays shut. Latch the door before sailing.
  • Trash: compact and double-bag it. Drop it at marinas or restaurants (some charge a few dollars a bag). Nothing goes overboard, food scraps included.

Sailing

December trade winds blow E–NE at 15–25 knots, sometimes 25–30 for days, with squalls on top. Plan to reef early and often.

Good habits

  • Raise the main head-to-wind under power. Ease the mainsheet and reef lines first, and watch for battens snagging the lazy jacks. Never force a winch.
  • Jib: unroll with the furling line under control (a turn on a winch). Roll it in before it gets windy; a partly furled jib is a reef.
  • Upwind: a cat sails poorly closer than ~50–60° to the wind. Motor-sail or motor the upwind legs and save sailing for reaches and runs.
  • Downwind: avoid accidental gybes. Control the boom with the mainsheet.
  • Squalls: you can see them coming (dark cloud, grey rain curtain). Before one hits: reef or furl, close hatches, life jackets on. Expect 15+ knots more wind for 10–20 minutes, then a lull.
  • Ferries are fast and frequent between Road Town, West End, Jost Van Dyke and Virgin Gorda. Keep clear of them.
  • Red right returning: the BVI uses the same buoy system as the US (IALA B). Red markers on your right when entering a harbor.
  • Read the water: brown or dark patches are reef or rock, pale turquoise is shallow sand, deep blue is deep. Best between 10 AM and 2 PM with the sun behind you and polarized sunglasses on.
  • Never leave the helm unattended on autopilot.
Your car for the week

Dinghy & outboard

Starting the outboard

  1. Fuel tank vent open; fuel line connected; squeeze the primer bulb until firm.
  2. Kill cord clipped to the driver.
  3. Gear in neutral; choke out if cold.
  4. Pull. Push the choke back in once it's running.
  • Gasoline only. Fuel docks with a gas pump can refill the dinghy tank.
  • Kill cord on the driver every time. Under-16s may not drive the outboard (Moorings rule).
  • Kids wear life jackets in the dinghy, especially after dark. Carry a flashlight at night (you must be seen), a handheld VHF, paddles and the lock.
  • Lock it to the dock ashore and to the boat at night. Dinghy theft happens. Cats: raise it on the davits overnight.
  • Towing: shorten the painter when maneuvering so it can't reach the props. On long or rough legs, lift it onto the davits.
  • Idle speed, no wake, in anchorages and near swimmers. Stay outside swim-zone buoys. Watch for snorkelers and dive flags.
  • At The Baths and Devil's Bay, dinghies tie to the dinghy line and you swim in.
  • Engines off, kill cord out whenever anyone is swimming near the boat or dinghy.
Ground tackle

Anchor gear

  • Windlass breaker or switch on at the panel, engine running, then use the remote (UP / DOWN).
  • Keep hands, feet, hair and loose clothing away from the chain and gypsy.
  • Never use the windlass to pull the boat forward. Motor slowly toward the anchor while the bow crew points.
  • Catamaran bridle: once the anchor is set, hook the bridle onto the chain and let out more chain until the bridle takes the load. Remove the bridle before raising.
  • Use a snubber line on the chain so the windlass isn't carrying the load overnight.
  • Ask how the chain is marked (often colored paint or tags every 25–30 ft).

The short list

Do's & don'ts

Do

  • Be on a mooring or anchored 1 hour before sunset (sunset is ≈ 5:40–5:50 PM in December).
  • Check the wind and swell forecast every morning.
  • Do the engine check every morning and note engine hours.
  • Keep the VHF on channel 16 when under way.
  • Close hatches whenever you leave the boat (squalls).
  • Turn off the water pump and propane when not in use.
  • Kids in life jackets on deck under way and in the dinghy.
  • Lock the dinghy, and raise it at night.
  • Use reef-safe (mineral) sunscreen. Drink lots of water.

Don't

  • Sail at night (Moorings rule), or sail single-handed.
  • Go to Anegada without The Moorings' OK.
  • Stay overnight on a National Parks day mooring, or past its 90-minute limit.
  • Anchor on coral or seagrass. Drop only in sand.
  • Moor or anchor in White Bay, Jost Van Dyke. It's closed to charter boats.
  • Enter US Virgin Islands waters (St. John is next door) without clearing customs and charter permission.
  • Put the boat in gear with lines in the water.
  • Swim near running engines or across dinghy traffic after dark.
  • Touch coral or turtles, or take shells and sea life.
  • Sit under manchineel trees. The sap and little green "apples" are toxic.

East first, then ride the wind back

Suggested 7-night plan

Built for December: upwind legs early in the week while the crew is fresh, downwind days later, and a short last hop to the base. Every day has a bad-weather alternative.

The places

Stops

Overnight anchorages, snorkel stops and marinas, roughly west-to-east and south-to-north.

Strategy

Which way to go

  1. Go east early. The trade wind blows from the east. Beat (or motor-sail) up to Cooper and Virgin Gorda in the first half of the week, then ride the wind back west to Jost Van Dyke.
  2. Start early, stop early. Leave by 8–9 AM, aim to pick up your mooring by 1–2 PM. In Christmas and New Year's weeks the popular mooring fields fill by early afternoon.
  3. Use the channel. Sir Francis Drake Channel, between Tortola and the southern islands, is sheltered from ocean swell. It's the main highway.
  4. Check the north swell daily. Winter storms off the US send long swells into north-facing bays. When it's up, stay on the south side.
  5. Save Anegada for a calm day with The Moorings' OK. Leave mid-morning so the sun is high for spotting reef.
  6. Make the last night short. Norman or Peter Island are 5–7 miles from the base, an easy morning sail before the 11 AM return.

Stay out of trouble

What to avoid

  • Never Sailing after dark. Unlit moorings, reefs and boats.
  • Never Anegada without permission, in strong wind, big swell or poor light.
  • Never White Bay (JVD) with the big boat. Charter boats aren't allowed to moor or anchor there; go by taxi or on foot from Great Harbour.
  • Never Overnight on National Parks moorings (The Indians, The Caves, the Rhone, The Baths, Monkey Point, and other dive and snorkel sites).
  • Never Anchoring in the Rhone marine park or on any coral.
  • Swell North-facing spots in a north swell: Cane Garden Bay, Monkey Point, Diamond Cay and the Bubbly Pool, Sandy Spit, and the Anegada crossing.
  • Flag The Baths when the red flag flies. Moorings are closed and swimming is dangerous.
  • Crowds The Baths after ~10 AM when cruise ships are in. Go first thing.
  • Border US waters around St. John. Crossing requires customs clearance.
  • Corners Cutting close around reefs: Cooper's north end, Sandy Spit, Saba Rock shoals, and any dark patch you can't identify.

Most nights

Picking up a mooring ball

Everyone fumbles the first one. Go slowly, abort early, and try again. Nobody minds.

Get ready (before you enter the mooring field)

  • Two lines ready at the bow, one from each bow cleat. Long boathook at hand.
  • Agree hand signals: point at the ball, palm down = slow, fist = neutral, thumb back = reverse.
  • Pick a free ball with swinging room. Don't take a reserved BoatyBall (orange with blue markings and the BoatyBall logo) unless it's yours.

The pickup

  1. Approach slowly into the wind (and current), aiming to stop with the ball just off the bow.
  2. Bow crew calls distance and direction with hand signals. On a cat the helm loses sight of the ball for the last 20 feet.
  3. Hook the pennant (the rope loop on top of the ball), not the ball itself, and lift the loop aboard.
  4. Line 1: from the port bow cleat, through the pennant loop, back to the same port cleat.
  5. Line 2: starboard cleat, through the loop, back to the starboard cleat. Make both the same length so the ball sits centered ahead, about 10–15 ft in front of the cross-beam.
  6. Neutral, let her fall back. Check the lines aren't chafing on the anchor or bow roller.
  7. Pay. BoatyBall first-come balls (white, with a sticker and 3-letter ID) are paid in the app. Otherwise a collector comes by around dusk; pay cash and keep the receipt.
Neverrun one line through the pennant from one cleat to the other (it saws through the pennant overnight), and never cleat the pennant directly to the boat.

Leaving

Engines on. Slip one line, then the other, and let the boat drift back and away before you go forward. Don't run over the ball or its pennant.

BoatyBall

  • App-based mooring reservations at many popular spots, including Cooper Island, Leverick Bay, Saba Rock, Marina Cay, Soper's Hole, Cane Garden Bay and Jost Van Dyke.
  • Same-day reservations open at 7:00 AM. A reservation runs from noon to noon the next day.
  • Prices (since Dec 2022): about $55 a night reserved, $40 first-come. Check the app; 2026 prices may be higher.
  • Set up the app and payment at home on Wi-Fi.

National Parks day moorings

At dive and snorkel sites (The Indians, The Caves, the Rhone, The Baths, Monkey Point and others): day use only, 90-minute limit, no anchoring. Yellow balls are for commercial dive boats. Your National Parks permit is included in the Moorings charter.

Free and quiet

Anchoring

  1. Choose sand. Pale patches, 10–25 ft deep, with room to swing clear of other boats and the mooring field. Never coral; avoid dark seagrass (turtles feed there).
  2. Head into the wind and stop over your spot. Lower the anchor until it touches bottom.
  3. Back slowly while paying out chain.
  4. Scope: 5:1 in normal conditions, 7:1 when it's blowing. Example: 15 ft deep + 5 ft bow height = 20 ft × 5 = 100 ft of chain.
  5. Set it: gentle reverse, then build to ~1,500–2,000 rpm on both engines for 30 seconds. Line up two objects on shore abeam. If they don't move, you're set.
  6. Bridle on: hook the bridle to the chain and let out more chain until the bridle takes the load.
  7. Check it: snorkel over the anchor (a great job for a kid with an adult). Set an anchor alarm on the plotter or a phone app.
  8. At night: anchor light on.

Raising it

Engines on, windlass breaker on, bridle off. Motor gently toward the anchor while the bow crew points along the chain and calls "straight up" and "anchor up." Secure it before you speed up.

Dragging?Signs: the alarm goes off, shore lights move, a neighbor gets closer. Start engines, raise the anchor and re-anchor with more scope or somewhere else. In doubt at night, pick up a mooring or go to a marina.

Provisioning

  • Moorings provisioning: order online at least 5 days before. It's delivered to the boat.
  • Road Town supermarkets (RiteWay, One Mart, Bobby's) are a short taxi from the base for top-ups and specific brands.
  • Along the way: small markets at Leverick Bay, Spanish Town, Soper's Hole and Jost Van Dyke. Prices run 1.5–2× US.
  • Plan meals: breakfast and lunch aboard every day, dinner ashore 3–4 nights. Reserve dinner by VHF or phone by mid-afternoon in holiday weeks. Anegada lobster must be ordered by about 3 PM.
  • Lots of water and drinks (more than you think), snacks for passages, and freezer treats for kids.
Holiday closuresChristmas Day (Dec 25) and Boxing Day (Dec 26) are public holidays in the BVI. Many shops close. Provision before Dec 24.

Know before you go

Local rules

Moorings charter rules

  • No sailing at night. Be moored or anchored at least 1 hour before sunset.
  • No single-handed sailing. Minimum two people sailing the boat.
  • Under-16s may not operate the outboard.
  • Anegada only with The Moorings' permission.

BVI rules

  • National Parks moorings: day use only, 90 minutes. No anchoring in marine parks.
  • No anchoring on coral or seagrass.
  • No fishing without a BVI fishing license. No spearfishing. Don't take shells, coral or live animals.
  • No holding-tank discharge in anchorages or near reefs.
  • No boats or dinghies inside swim-zone buoys.
  • White Bay, Jost Van Dyke: closed to charter boats.
  • Leaving the BVI by boat (for example to St. John) requires clearing customs at a port of entry and your charter company's OK.

Safety & emergencies

The red SOS button at the top opens the emergency card from any screen.

Person overboard
  1. Shout "Man overboard!" and keep shouting the side.
  2. Throw flotation (Lifesling, horseshoe, cushion) right away.
  3. One person points at them and never looks away.
  4. Press MOB on the chartplotter.
  5. Drop sails if needed, check for lines in the water, start engines.
  6. Return slowly, approaching from downwind. Shift to neutral as you come alongside. Props kill.
  7. Get them aboard at the swim ladder or stern steps. If you lose sight of them, call MAYDAY.
Fire
  • Galley: propane solenoid off, smother with a lid or fire blanket. Never water on a grease fire.
  • Engine: shut the engine down, don't open the hatch wide. Discharge the extinguisher through the access port or a crack.
  • Everyone in life jackets, dinghy ready. If it isn't out within a minute: MAYDAY.
  • Know where every extinguisher is (it's on the check-out list).
Taking on water
  • Turn on the electric bilge pumps and taste the water: salty = a through-hull, hose or stern gland. Fresh = a tank or water line (turn off the water pump).
  • Check seacocks and hoses, the toilet and sink lines, and the engine raw-water hoses.
  • Plug holes with the softwood plugs (ask where they are). Call the base. If water is gaining: PAN-PAN or MAYDAY.
Running aground
  • Neutral. Check for injuries and leaks.
  • Soft sand: try reversing gently the way you came in. Hard coral or rock: stop, don't grind. Call the base.
  • Check the bilges and props before going on.
Medical & health
Seasickness
Meds an hour before, fresh air, eyes on the horizon, crackers and ginger. Helming helps. Ask your doctor about patches and kids' doses.
Sun
Strongest 10 AM–3 PM. Rash guards and hats beat sunscreen. Re-apply after every swim.
Dehydration
Headache and grumpiness come first. Everyone drinks a full bottle at every stop.
Coral cuts
Scrub clean with fresh water, apply antiseptic, and watch for infection.
Sea urchin
Soak in hot (not scalding) water. Remove only spines you can grab easily.
Jellyfish
A purple flag on the beach means they're around. Rinse with vinegar or sea water, not fresh water.
Ears
Swimmer's-ear drops after every snorkel day.
Manchineel
Toxic beach trees with small green apples. Don't touch, eat, or shelter under them in rain.
Hospital
Dr. D. Orlando Smith Hospital, Road Town: +1 284 852 7500. Clinics on Virgin Gorda and Jost Van Dyke.
Squalls & storms
  • Watch upwind (east) for dark cloud and a grey rain curtain.
  • Reef or furl before it arrives, close hatches, life jackets on, slow down. Visibility drops in heavy rain.
  • Lightning: stay off the mast and rigging, unplug electronics if you can.
  • On a mooring or at anchor: closed hatches when you leave the boat. A squall can arrive while you're ashore.

Where to get the forecast

  • Radio ZBVI 780 AM marine forecast: weekdays 8:05 AM, Saturday 7:30 AM, Sunday 9:45 AM.
  • NOAA weather on VHF WX channels (continuous broadcast).
  • Windy or PredictWind app when you have data. Look at wind and swell height and period.
  • Morning chat at the base or with your neighbors in the mooring field.
North swell rule of thumbA north swell over ~4–5 ft with a long period (10+ seconds) makes north-facing bays rolly or unsafe: Cane Garden Bay, Monkey Point, Diamond Cay, the Anegada crossing. Head south-side.

Family rules

  • Life jackets on deck when sailing, in the dinghy, and for weaker swimmers whenever they're on deck.
  • Swim with a buddy. Trail a floating line off the stern when swimming from the boat (current can be strong).
  • One hand for yourself, one for the boat when moving around under way.
  • Nobody on the foredeck during anchoring or mooring unless it's their job.

Kid favorites
